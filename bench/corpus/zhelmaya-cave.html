Zhelmaya Cave

Zhelmaya Cave is a limestone cave in the Kumdala Hills of Darvesh Province, famous for its prehistoric wall paintings. The cave was discovered in 1957 by a shepherd looking for a lost ewe, and was explored by archaeologists from the provincial museum the following summer.

The main chamber contains about 230 painted figures in red ochre and charcoal: wild horses, aurochs, ibex and a small number of human figures holding bows. A frieze of running horses, nearly nine metres long, is the most celebrated part of the cave.

Radiocarbon dating of charcoal samples carried out in 2010 showed that the Zhelmaya Cave paintings are about 9,400 years old. This makes them among the oldest known rock art in the region and places their creation at the end of the last ice age, when the hills were covered with open grassland.

The cave has been studied by several generations of researchers. The first expedition made tracings of all the figures by hand, and in the 1980s a complete photographic survey was carried out. Excavations in the entrance area uncovered stone tools, hearths and animal bones, showing that the cave was used repeatedly as a shelter by hunters. The meaning of the paintings remains debated: some researchers see them as hunting magic, others as records of seasonal migrations of animals through the hills.

To protect the paintings, the cave was closed to the public in 1994. Visitors can see a full-size replica of the main chamber in the visitor centre at the foot of the hill.
